## Podcast

### Segment 1

Now, this is the first of three segments of smaller stories from today that did not make the main list, and the first one is a good reminder that not every AI story is really about AI.

It starts with Proton, the privacy company many people recommend when you want to get off Gmail. This week 404 Media published an investigation into how Proton's leadership is pushing AI inside the company. The detail that made the rounds came from the security Slack channel. An engineer asked for a few days to pentest new code before shipping it. That just means deliberately attacking your own software to find weak spots before someone else does.

According to the reporting, CEO Andy Yen answered that this was "bullshit and loser thinking", and told the team to stop lovingly handcrafting code and use AI more. Staff were also told their AI usage could decide whether they kept their jobs. When that plan was dropped after it leaked, Yen said it had already served its purpose.

Now, the writer of the post we are looking at, a long-time Proton customer, says that for the first time he is not sure he would still recommend them. And his argument is interesting. Many reactions treat this as an AI story, as if any AI-written code poisons the well. He says that misses the point.

His view is that engineers using AI to write code is the new norm, and it is fine as long as there are guardrails. Someone reviews what the model produced, it gets tested, and someone is accountable for what ships. What the reporting describes is a CEO telling an engineer, in front of colleagues, that raising a security concern was "loser thinking". That is a leadership problem, he says, not an AI problem.

And honestly, I think the privacy angle makes it sharper. Imagine your bank saying it will ship faster and test less. You would be nervous, right? With Proton, what people pay for is the confidence that the builders are careful. The writer says a bug exposing users' mail would be fatal for a company like this.

One more detail. He says an employee defending the company on Reddit claimed Yen was fine with pentesting, as long as it did not slow down the pipeline. He reads that as clumsy crisis management. That is his opinion, and it is worth remembering it is one customer's read, not a proven fact. Still, the test he sets is simple: does the engineer asking for a few more days of testing still win the argument?

From a company worrying about code to a story about AI watching people. In the United Kingdom, Futurism, citing TechRadar, reports a surge in customers wrongly accused of shoplifting by AI facial recognition systems in retail stores. TechRadar highlighted a number of recent cases across the country, where shoppers say the false accusations left them shaken.

One woman, identified as Mary, said her husband has been socially "isolated" after a quick trip into a Home Bargains store for vitamins. The store's system flagged him as a shoplifter, and the couple was made to leave by management, seemingly without any chance to dispute it. "I was scared to go into a shop," he told TechRadar.

In another case, a man said his wife was "shaking from head to toe" after being removed from a Budgens store for stealing "loads of meat". The twist is that they had not set foot in that store for months.

So how do you clear your name? In the UK you can file a Subject Access Request, or SAR, which is like a Freedom of Information request but aimed at private companies. The route exists, but it is a massive undertaking, and it means handing over even more private information. Jack Coulson of the civil liberty group Big Brother Watch called it "the stuff of Kafkaesque nightmares to be trapped in a maze of bureaucracy and forced to hand over even more personal data just to be told the accusation."

Think about the everyday side of this. Say you pop into a shop for a quick errand and a screen decides you are a thief. You are the one who has to prove otherwise. My read is that the real issue is not only the false match, it is that there is no easy way back for the person accused.

Staying with AI making judgements about people, the last story in this segment is about prisons. Michigan is set to deploy an AI system across its prisons to classify incarcerated people based on "how dangerous they are", according to Detroit channel Fox2.

Here is the background. In Michigan, incarcerated people are separated into five housing levels by security classification. Until now, screenings were done by a committee of staff appointed by the Warden at each facility. Now the Michigan Department of Corrections wants that decision heavily informed by software from a non-profit tech company called Recidiviz, which began as a data organization project at Google.

The department says it will use the software to "optimize placement of incarcerated individuals", starting with a pilot in a number of men's facilities beginning in December. It also says the tool is expected to "improve MDOC's ability to more accurately predict incarcerated individuals' risk of future violence by approximately 30 percent". That is the department's own claim, and Futurism points out it is unclear how the state arrived at that figure.

Why does it matter? Housing level decides which programmes and resources a person can get, and what kind of population they live with for the whole sentence. So a wrong label has long consequences.

Futurism raises a warning from Louisiana. A ProPublica investigation into a similar risk-analysis program there found the data used contained inherent racial and socioeconomic bias. In one case, a blind, wheelchair-bound 70-year-old was rated a "moderate risk" of reoffending despite a clean disciplinary record, which meant an automatic loss of parole eligibility.

To be fair, Michigan's system is aimed at housing, not parole. And Futurism says Michigan's department was contacted for comment. What data gets collected will matter a great deal. So the thing to watch is whether that is made public once the December pilot begins.

### Segment 2

Let's stay with the physical side of AI for a moment, because it keeps coming back to one thing: power. First, the UK. National Grid has taken a step towards building Britain's biggest electricity substation, on Teesside.

Last week, the company agreed to buy 29.5 hectares, that's 73 acres, of land at Dorman Point, on a site owned by Teesworks, a local private-public partnership. The deal was first reported by Sky News. The substation could supply power to a vast neighbouring datacentre. Reports last year said the UK government had picked Teesworks as the site for a new AI datacentre, and planning applications had been submitted for a project spanning nearly 500,000 sq metres.

Matt Pearce, National Grid's head of land, said the deal reflects constructive work between everyone involved and will support the growth of AI and digital technologies. And Ben Houchen, the mayor of the Tees Valley combined authority, was blunt with Sky News. He said everyone who has tried to build a datacentre in the country knows the biggest problem is getting enough power to the site quickly.

He said having up to 3.2GW of capacity there means the region can finally compete for these projects. And he'd much rather the data sat on Teesside than in Texas or Abu Dhabi. Now, that's a politician making a pitch, so take it as his view. But the underlying point is interesting. The chip is not the only bottleneck. Sometimes it's simply the wire.

Imagine you want to open a huge restaurant. You find the perfect building, you hire the chefs, and then the city says the gas line will take years. That's roughly the problem here. The substation is the gas line.

The government is clearly leaning in. Last week it published a draft national policy statement on datacentres, describing them as, quote, as fundamental to our economy as the railways and electricity networks on which we have long depended. It also said the country and host communities must benefit from them.

But there's pushback. The Guardian notes strong opposition to datacentres in the UK, with campaign groups protesting in places from Auchtertool, a village north of Edinburgh, to Brick Lane in east London. And in October, the Guardian reported that AI companies told ministers they had 12 months to head off public opposition, or risk US-style protests that could threaten construction.

One more detail. Balfour Beatty was reported to be in line to deliver the substation, with Steel River Power also heavily involved. Both were approached for comment. So this is a step, not a finished build. My read is that this story matters mostly as a signal: power access is now the thing that decides where AI gets built.

And that leads neatly to the cost of that power. A new analysis by the Sierra Club, first reported by Bloomberg, looks at the United States. It says that if currently planned AI developments enter operation as scheduled, the US energy grid is expected to emit 2.1 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide by 2030.

That would be a 42 percent rise over the five-year period, an expected increase of 618 million tons compared to 2025. Futurism's framing is that it would undo nearly 15 years of pollution reduction efforts. Jeremy Fisher, the Sierra Club's principal advisor for climate and energy, told Bloomberg, quote, The numbers continue to sort of boggle our minds.

Why gas? Because of major bottlenecks in the electrical grid, many developers have chosen on-site or near-site power plants, and those are overwhelmingly fuelled by fossil fuels. Futurism points to Elon Musk's 1.2 gigawatt data center near Memphis, which it says is already affecting residents with fleets of methane generators. It adds that legal experts say these flout local permitting and federal environmental rules. That's Futurism's account, and I'd treat it as such.

Also notice this is an analysis by an environmental advocacy group, and it depends on those planned projects actually going ahead on schedule. Not all of them will. Futurism itself says the number of projects at risk of cancellation is rising, driven by grassroots organizing and financial pressure.

Put the two stories side by side and you get the real tension. One place is racing to connect more power to AI. Another is counting the emissions that come with it. Honestly, if you follow only one thing, follow where the power comes from. That will tell you more about AI's real cost than any benchmark.

### Segment 3

Let's shift from power to something more personal: what you hear and see on a live broadcast. Microsoft has filed a patent for an AI system that could alter live video in near real time, changing a speaker's words and even their face and mouth to match. A patent is only a filing, not a product, and Microsoft hadn't responded to a request for comment when Futurism reported it.

The patent was first flagged by a patent-tracker called Patentlyze. Its inventors describe it as near real-time AI enhancement of video or augmented reality presentations of live events. AI agents would find certain words, then alter or remove them, and edit the speaker's face and mouth to fit. They'd work from set parameters or user profiles, and they'd also smooth the video and audio just before and after the change, so the edit doesn't show.

Now, some of the uses are quite sensible. One is live translation, where you both hear and see a broadcast in your preferred language. Another is removing filler words like "um" and "ah", or erasing stutters. Imagine you watch a lecture where the professor says "um" every few seconds. A clean version might be genuinely nice.

But the patent goes further. It talks about cutting "coined terms" and colloquial terms, with "y'all" and "bite the bullet" as examples. It also mentions removing mumbled, mispronounced, misused, misspoken, offensive, insensitive or inappropriate words. And replacement words, it says, may come from an "internet search".

Here's what makes it interesting. Today, if you don't like what someone is saying, you switch it off. This system describes something different: you adjust their speech to your taste instead. Futurism asks whether that pushes us further into personalized bubbles, and what's so bad about hearing someone from the South say "y'all". Honestly, that's a fair question.

To be fair, the patent does mention that the original live video and audio may be recorded and stored as "ground truth". That's a nod to the risk of misuse and confusion. The kinds of events it lists include lectures, presentations, awards ceremonies, game and cooking shows, and live sports.

My read is this: it's a patent, so don't panic. But it's worth watching, because the phrase "live broadcast" quietly depends on everyone seeing the same thing. If two viewers get different versions of the same speech, that shared reality gets thin.

Now, a story about what AI takes in, rather than what it puts out. In Australia, the Pitjantjatjara artist Sally Scales says AI is taking First Nations art and turning it into "Temu slop". Scales chairs the National Gallery of Australia's First Nations advisory group. She calls it "a theft" of cultural knowledge, and says it undermines the agency of artists and the authenticity of their work, as The Guardian reports.

There's a policy backdrop. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese has vowed to protect Australian creatives from copyright theft. Attorney general Michelle Rowland has said there are "no plans to weaken copyright protections". Yet the report says AI has already been trained, without compensation, on the works of well known Australian artists including Kylie Minogue and Nick Cave.

Experts are especially worried about Indigenous cultural and intellectual property, because AI companies can hoover up anything found online, including songs and paintings that carry sacred and cultural knowledge. Searches on Temu, Shein and Amazon turn up countless works described as Aboriginal paintings, plus coasters and stubby holders with Aboriginal-style designs, mostly with no artist credited. Scales says she looks at some works and wonders whether they ever went through a First Nations community or person, or were just "whipped up".

Her point is that the artist's family lineage and lived experience aren't there. Prof Kathy Bowrey, an intellectual property expert at UNSW Sydney, says one in five creators already had copyright problems without AI. It gets more complex for First Nations creators. Her example: a corporate client pays once for artwork or images of Aboriginal people, then later uses AI to "update" them without permission. Then, she says, artists lose repeat business and compete against an AI when they make new work.

The peak body for visual artists, the National Association for the Visual Arts, or Nava, raised this in a submission to a joint select committee inquiry on AI. It cited lawyer Terri Janke, who has documented AI scraping Indigenous art centre websites, removing artists' names and altering their work. Nava says AI-generated "Aboriginal-style" images can be sold as Indigenous or Indigenous-inspired, causing cultural and economic harm.

So what's the fix? Bowrey and Associate Prof Oliver Bown have proposed a "copyright plus" framework. AI companies would have to disclose their data sources, and artists would get the right to say no to scraping or mimicry. They also propose a levy on AI companies to pay creative professionals and fund a cultural scheme, plus takedown notices for fake content.

The attorney general's spokesperson said the government is still consulting creators, media organisations and AI companies on options for "meaningful control and fair compensation". So nothing is decided yet. If you buy art, especially anything labelled Aboriginal, my read is it's worth checking whether an artist is actually named.
